# The Fenyuan Generation — hero tales (English drafts, second pass)

These are working drafts. The card text is retold in Chinese from them, not translated line by line. Where the Chinese and the English differ, the Chinese is the one that goes into the card.

Names used below: Grey Ridge (灰岭), the Blackstone Highland (黑石高地), the Greenwheat plain (青麦原), Granary City (麦仓城), Twinbridge (双桥城), Millstone City (石磨城), Molten Heart (熔铁炉心), the Dragonbone Heap (龙骨堆), Underlid (盖下), Old Lid (老盖), Myren (麦伦), Dagny (达格妮), Kumironi (库米罗妮, harvest), Luluvie (璐璐薇, wind). The dragon is Fenyuan, the Burning, later sung as the Crownless Dragon (无冠之龙).

## The four armies, by Myren's tally stick

Myren of Granary City walked back alive from all four armies sent against the dragon. Each time, he sat down by the gate and cut one notch into half a tally stick for every man and woman who had not come back.

- Late autumn 1690. Seventeen notches. Connor's band of ten hunters from the snow line, and the lord of Twinbridge's son Edwin with six riders. Only Edwin's horse came home, with Myren walking behind it.
- Spring 1691. Forty-one notches, thirteen of them side by side: Oswin of Grey Ridge and his twelve old sworn men.
- Autumn 1691. Twenty-five notches. This army went out because Fia had made the dragon bleed.
- Autumn 1692, mustered at Millstone after Twinbridge burned. Thirty-one notches. The first was for Cadoc.

## Oswin of Grey Ridge, the King of the Empty Seat

"By the fire of Grey Ridge and the crown of my fathers I swear it. I go down to the wheat country and take the fire out of that dragon's heart and set it in the third seat of this crown. If I do not bring it, I do not come back to this hall, I do not sit in this chair, I do not drink one drop of its ale."

Grey Ridge was the smallest of the iron towns on the eastern rim of the Blackstone Highland, a day's climb above the Greenwheat plain, and it had the longest hall on the highland: stand at the door and you could not see the fire at the far end. Its crown was a ring of black iron with three seats. Two held beast-cores, cut by Oswin's grandfather and his father from beasts they had slain with their own hands. The third seat was empty, and it was his. That was the rule of Grey Ridge: a king's son sets his own kill into the crown, or he does not wear it.

From eight to fourteen he was fostered in Granary City. Grey Ridge sold iron for Greenwheat grain, and the two houses traded children to keep the bargain honest. He learned to read tally sticks, to judge wheat by the smell, to drink the thin Greenwheat ale, and for six years he called the lord of Granary City "Father."

At fifteen, the autumn after the thunder season, the leader of a thunder-mane wolf pack came down off the high pastures to drag cattle. Oswin stood on the wall in the dark. Other people saw nothing out there. He saw a light, the beast's core burning in its chest like a lamp in a window, three hundred paces off. He threw. At dawn they found the wolf in a ditch with his javelin through the light. No one in three generations of his family had killed so young. He gave the core away the same morning, to the herdsman whose cows had been taken, because the man had lost more than Oswin had won. The third seat stayed empty. His father laughed until he coughed.

He grew into a generous king's son, generous past sense. On the highland a king is measured by how freely he gives; Oswin gave his food to refugees, his arm-rings to anyone who told a good story, and his promises to everyone. He loved riddles. "The more I am wounded, the richer my master. On the night the fire comes, I die before him." The answer was a tally stick. Whoever guessed won an arm-ring; whoever failed drank a horn of ale. Most of his rings went that way.

In the autumn of 1690 a red dragon landed on the wall of Granary City and told the people to call it king. The lord of Granary called it king. It burned the city anyway, because, it said, there was no fear in the word. The lord was the man Oswin had called Father. That night in the long hall Oswin put his hand on the fire-blackened oath stone and swore, and before the cup went round he stripped three rings off his arms and gave them to the three men sitting farthest from the fire. A boast made at a feast still holds when the sun comes up.

At the gate in the morning stood his father's twelve old sworn men. The youngest was forty-three. The oldest, Wulf, had one eye and a white beard. They had sworn at his father's fire: where the lord's son goes, we go. Oswin told them to go home. They said his father had told them to follow him. He told them again. Wulf put a javelin in his hand. "Walk. We need to be off the mountain by dark."

That was what weighed on him. A sworn man does not leave the field alive if his lord dies on it. His oath was taking him to a dragon, and it was taking twelve old men with him. He said he was going for the empty seat. The old men knew he was going for the man he had called Father.

On the riverbank under Twinbridge he wanted to give everyone something. He offered Hob a ring to come up to Grey Ridge; Hob would not. He shouted at Cadoc all night across a fire, one of them wanting to cut out the dragon's heart, the other wanting to call it home, and then gave Cadoc a ring, which Cadoc would not take. He raced Fia along the riverbank for a ring and lost by thirty paces. He offered the lord's hound Ronan a ring to come up the mountain as a sworn man; Ronan said a hound keeps one gate, and Oswin gave him the ring anyway. "I give it because you're worth it."

In the spring of 1691 the second army went out, forty-two strong, and Oswin and his twelve led it. When the dragon rested between breaths, the scale under its throat went dark, and Oswin saw the core burning behind it. He threw, and hit, and did not go through. The twelve stood in a ring around his body and none of them stepped back. Wulf fell last. He shouted at the dragon in the Grey Ridge tongue: "You have no crown. We have a king." Myren came back, the only one. Among the forty-one notches he cut that day, thirteen sit side by side.

The third seat of the Grey Ridge crown was never filled. The kings after him wore the crown with its empty seat and said they were keeping it for him, and the poets called him the King of the Empty Seat. In the winter of 1693 the last of the Greenwheat people came up the highland with nothing. They went to the big iron towns first, and the big towns shut their gates. Grey Ridge was the smallest town on the rim, and the last, and on the ninety-seventh day it opened, because the boy had died for these people. It fed them to the end of winter and in spring showed them the empty mining towns to the north. Grey Ridge itself held on another eighty-some years. His riddles outlived his town. Highland children were still asking each other about the tally stick thousands of years later, and nobody remembered whose riddle it was.

## Inga of Molten Heart, who broke three swords

"How old were you that year?"
"Seventeen."
"Nobody can get into the crater."
"I did."

Dwarf babies grab for a hammer first. Inga crushed the rail of her cradle. By her early teens she had snapped an ironwood bar in her grip and ground raw ore to powder in her palm. Every smith in Molten Heart who forged for her watched the work fail: hilts bent in her grip, hammer-heads split. One old smith made her three blades. When the third broke in her hand, he put out his own forge in front of the whole street and said he would not take the work. In a city whose first law is that the forges never go out, a smith quenching his fire is a disgrace worse than death. After that no smith would make anything for her.

Molten Heart judges people by their work, not their blood. A dwarf is what he has made. Inga was the strongest person in the city and the only one who had never set a piece before the anvil. Only Dagny wanted her. Dagny, of the line that kept the first forge, was trying to make a metal that could kill a dragon, and she was famous for a temper harder than slag. When Inga was nine, Dagny took her to the bellows of the first forge, a bellows bigger than a house with a lever of solid black iron that three grown dwarves could barely move, and slapped the lever. "Wind, at least, you can't break."

For six years Inga worked those bellows. Each stroke turned the fire one shade whiter. Every attempt Dagny made in those six years was fired by her.

Nobody believed what she said. She said she had not lost a wrestling bout since she was nine; nobody believed it until no one would wrestle her before Doan's Anvil on Oath-Hammer Day. She said the fire had to burn white into blue; only Dagny believed her. She said she could hear the stone moving inside the mountain at night; the city laughed for a year, until a level of the mine came down and she had cleared everyone out of it half a day before. Rolf, who was born the same year on the same street and had the sharpest tongue in the city, spent it on her, and was always the first to stand beside her afterwards.

She fought with her hands. What she feared was touching people. Dwarves greet each other by clasping forearms; she pressed her fist to her own chest instead. Strangers thought she was proud. Her friends knew she was afraid of breaking an arm. She wanted two things: something she could not break, and a hand she could safely hold.

Dagny's metal lacked one last ingredient. Dagny never said what. Inga decided it was the bone of a true dragon. Three days north of the city, in the mountains, there is a crater the old songs call the Dragonbone Heap, and they say the bottom of it is bones. The air that comes up out of it would soften iron. It is forbidden ground; dwarves dig down, never up.

In the winter of 1692 into 1693 she was seventeen. She went up alone. When she came back she said she had jumped into the crater, wrestled all night with a golden, winged shadow, and woken with a bone in her hands. She ran down the mountain with it. She reached the first forge the morning after Dagny had thrown herself into the fire on the eleven-thousand-and-first attempt. She was one night late. From that day the first forge was called the Mother Forge.

The bone became a sword. She never sharpened it. Years later Rolf said it in front of a full room: "The crater is forbidden, nobody can get in." "I got in." "And none of us has ever seen you draw that sword." "Don't need to."

She lived three hundred years. Around 1720 a siege-beast came against the gate of Grey Ridge, and she broke its jaw with her bare hands while the garrison watched from the wall. She dug seven people out of eleven from under a fallen rock layer. In three hundred years she never lost a wrestling bout. On the day she died she drew the sword once, and the people around her saw that it really was dragon bone. Whether she ever went into the crater, nobody can prove either way. The sword is Unground Bone: true dragon bone, enough to carry a legend, but everything she was known for she did with her hands, and the deed was never set into it. Dwarf children learned to talk back with her three words, and the grown-ups scolded them, and then laughed.

## Hob of Millstone City, the Snow Granary

"Kumironi, one more granary. Just one. Give it, and tomorrow morning I'll sweep your shrine. Three times. No, twice. I'll never get up for three."

Millstone City stood under the cliff at the north-east end of the Greenwheat plain, where the river came down off the rock and turned dozens of water mills. Hob's family had the seventh. He was the youngest at the mill and the sleepiest, flour in his hair, freckles on his face, short for fifteen, smelling of new flour wherever he went.

When he was seven, rust got into the family's wheat. He knelt at the edge of the field and asked Kumironi, the harvest goddess, to take it away, and in the morning it was gone. From then on, anything he asked for that fell within the harvest was answered. In those years nobody knew that gods walked among people. Prayers were answered sometimes and sometimes not. Hob's were answered every time, so the town called him the wheat-god's boy, half as a joke and half out of fear. The temple's chief priest could not decide whether he was a saint or a witch and made him kneel at the shrine every tenth day; he fell asleep kneeling every time. His mother said: don't ask so much, whatever you ask for has to be paid back.

The goddess dealt only in what grows in the field, what is stored in the granary and what is cooked in the pot. An empty granary could be filled once a night; a field could ripen half a month early; bread could rise without yeast; a cow that could not calve could be eased. Nothing else. He asked her once not to let a man die, and she did not answer. He asked her to stop a fire, and she did not answer. He cursed her and then went back to asking for wheat. She never answered anything he asked for himself: a dry day for a nap, a neighbour girl looking his way. "The god doesn't owe me," he said. "I owe her."

Along the field ridges, wherever he went, there was a gleaning girl, sunburnt, a wheat ear in her braid. Hob swore she was Kumironi. The girl laughed: "Have you ever seen a god?" Everyone else saw a poor girl picking up dropped ears.

When Granary City burned, the lord of Millstone sent Hob down to Twinbridge with the grain carts. Thousands of people from Granary were camped on the river beach below the south bridge. He knelt among the empty carts, made his offer, lay down on an empty sack, and in the morning the carts were full.

Everyone wanted him. The lord of Twinbridge wanted him with the army, because an army that eats is worth more than a hundred knights. The temple wanted him kept at the shrine. Oswin offered an arm-ring if he would come to Grey Ridge. Hob wanted to go home to the seventh mill, sleep on the flour sacks and listen to the millstone all day. He was afraid of fire; the smell of smoke sent him to the river. This war was against a fire dragon.

He haggled with everything: with the goddess, with people, with beasts. Once a wolf cornered him behind the mill and he talked wheat prices at it until it left. When someone came to him bleeding, he gave them bread first and then said, "She only does wheat. You're bleeding. Go to someone else." When someone called him a saint, he waved it off: "Don't. Saints have to get up early." If anyone in the camp had not eaten, he could not sleep. He took bread to the lord's hound at the south gate every evening, because the boy never refused food.

In 1693, before the dragon came over Millstone, he went home and led everyone who was left out through the waterway at the foot of the cliff behind the mill. All three cities were gone, the kingdom was gone. That winter, the day before the people went up onto the highland, he knelt on the red soil at the edge of the burnt plain and asked for wheat, and for the first time in his life there was no answer. Nothing would grow in what the dragon had burned, not even for her.

Forty-three thousand people climbed onto the Blackstone Highland in the dead of winter. No single iron town could feed forty thousand mouths, and one by one they shut their gates. The people walked along the rim from town to town for ninety-seven days. Every empty cart was full the next morning, and Ragna walked ahead of them listening for water under the snow. No one starved. On the ninety-seventh day Grey Ridge opened its gate. The poets called him the Snow Granary, because the carts filled even in snow. The Greenwheat people settled in the empty towns of the north, and their grandchildren's grandchildren still break a loaf for passing strangers on midwinter day, to pay back that winter.

Legends do not grow old. For thousands of years afterwards he planted along the edge of the red plain, one row at a time. Nothing would grow in the red itself, but the edge had given nothing either, and in the age of the kingdoms the villages there reaped half of what a field elsewhere would give. The old people say that half was his asking, one row at a time.

## Ragna of the High Pastures, who heard water under stone

"Nobody called me. I came."

Ragna was a shepherd's daughter from the high pastures above Grey Ridge. She went barefoot all year, the soles of her feet hard as horn, and she knew every spring on the ridge. She could lay her ear against rock and hear water running underneath.

When she was six, an old shepherd with no sheep came over the pass. She gave him her whole lunch. He watched her lying with her ear to a rock. "There's more water under the stone than on top of it," he said, and went on over the next ridge. She saw him again every few years, always on the ridge, never any older.

When she was ten, a drought dried the high pastures. She walked all day until she heard water under a slab of grey rock, struck it with her crook, and a spring came out. The flock lived. The spring never dried. Grey Ridge half believed her and called her the water-listener.

Before Oswin was sent down to the plain, they herded together. She taught him the sling; he taught her riddles. When he came back at fourteen he wore arm-rings and spoke like a prince, and she still called him Strawstalk. At fifteen, after the wolf, he gave her the first ring he had ever given anyone. She wore it on her ankle, because she was always barefoot.

In the autumn of 1690 the water under the ridge tasted of ash. Nobody called her. She followed Oswin's band down to the plain, because the water was telling her something was wrong down there, and because she had always wanted to follow the water to wherever it went. She had never seen the sea. On the plain she raced Fia twice: up a slope Ragna won, across the stubble Fia won. Ragna said soft ground was cheating.

The night before the second army marched, Oswin sent her back to the camp to find water for the morning and left her a riddle. "I have no feet and I'm always walking, no mouth and I'm always talking, and where I've been the grass is green." She had the answer before he finished: water. He said it didn't count, she could tell him when he got back. He did not come back. She carried his spear up the highland to Grey Ridge, and the long hall took down a sea-coloured sword that had hung on its wall for three generations, and gave it to her for bringing the spear home.

In the winter of 1693 the Greenwheat people came up onto the highland with nothing. For ninety-seven days she walked ahead of forty-three thousand people, listening for water under snow and stone; Hob kept the carts full behind her. No one died of thirst.

About 1706 the water in the valley under the ridge began to drop, until the bottoms of the wells showed stone. Something had coiled itself over the spring. What happened next is sung two ways, by the people on the ridge and the people in the valley, and the two sides have despised each other ever since.

The ridge sings it like this. "You're going down?" "There are still people in the valley." The valley people never once climbed up to see her; in a drought they came up and shouted for her and went back down. She never waited to be called. When the water dropped, she simply went. She fought three days and three nights. The sword cracked halfway and did not break. She let the water back into the valley and walked out of the valley mouth with her head up, and never once looked back. "Was it worth it?" "The valley has water."

The valley sings it another way. "You're going down?" "That thing has something I want." The ridge people looked down on the valley, drought or no drought. When the water dropped she came down on her own; nobody called her, and she never asked whether anyone wanted her. It took her one night. The sword cracked halfway and did not break. She dug out the thing's heart and took it with her; the water came back on its own once the thing was dead. She walked out of the valley mouth with her head down, walking fast. "Was it worth it?" She did not answer.

Both songs agree on this much: she went down alone, the thing died, the water came back, and after she walked out of the valley mouth no one ever saw her again. Her sword was found driven deep into the rock at the valley mouth, with a scale set in its guard that had come off the thing. The ridge says it was a dragon. The valley says it was a very big fish. The ridge says she left the sword for the valley; the valley says she threw it away. That is Valley-Mouth Scale. Neither song ever says her name.

## Cadoc of Underlid, who drove the cattle up the mountain

"Old Lid. Come home."

Underlid was a village at the foot of a volcano in the southern belt. For centuries a red dragon had lain across the mountain's mouth with its chin on the crater's rim, and the village believed that as long as it lay there the mountain would not wake. They called it Old Lid. Every autumn, in the month of falling leaves, they drove one ox and one cart of salt up the mountain, and Old Lid took them and did not come down.

Each generation one child of Cadoc's family drove the tribute up. On that child's first drive, the dragon let a drop of its blood fall on the child's tongue, so that it would know the child's smell. Children born in a family that had tasted dragon blood for centuries did not burn; Cadoc, as a toddler, picked coals out of the hearth and played with them. He also heard, from birth, what every beast called itself in its own heart. Old Lid called itself Old Lid. It had taken the village's name for its own.

He drove the tribute for seven autumns, from nine to sixteen. Old Lid talked to him. It asked about the village and the taste of the salt. In a snowstorm once it let him sleep against its jaw, and the scale was warm.

In the autumn of 1690 his cart broke a wheel on the mountain path and he was half a day late. When he reached the crater, Old Lid did not look at the ox. It was looking north-east. It said that the seven kings were lying down, and a dragon ought to have a king. Cadoc listened for its name and heard a different one now. It called itself King. It flew. That night the mountain woke, and Underlid lay under the lava by morning. Cadoc was still on the path with the ox and the salt.

He dragged them north-east for forty days, following the smoke, and reached the river beach at Twinbridge among the people of Granary City. The hunters there laughed at a boy who meant to pay tribute to a dragon. "The bargain was made in autumn," he said. "Autumn isn't over." Hob asked him for the ox to feed the camp. He said it belonged to Old Lid. In the winter a child in the camp could not get warm and would not eat, and Cadoc led the ox to Hob himself.

He believed that if it heard its old name it would remember, and come home. Oswin wanted to cut out its heart for his crown, and the two of them shouted at each other across a fire all night. Cadoc did not march with the second army. He said it hadn't heard its old name yet; nobody should cut out its heart before that.

Twinbridge burned in the summer of 1692, and that autumn the lord of Millstone raised the fourth army below the cliff, where the red soil had spread down from the top. At dawn, in the month of falling leaves, Cadoc walked out ahead of the line dragging the cart of salt, stood on the red line and called it. "Old Lid. Come home." The whole army saw it: the great head came down toward him, and stopped, for one breath, perhaps two. His gift could not have done that; nothing his size could stop a thing that size. It stopped on its own. Then the dragon said, "Call me king." Its fire was half red and half black. The red did not burn him. The black did. Myren came back, the only one, and the first of the thirty-one notches he cut that day was for Cadoc.

Where he stood, the salt from the cart burned into the soil. There is a white line a few paces long across the red plain at the foot of the cliff, and it has not faded. On the highland, the old Greenwheat families say "call it by its old name" when they mean giving someone one last chance to remember who they were.

## Fia, who was given to the wind

"Wind, if she lands alive, she's yours, not mine."

Fia's father hunted on the south-west edge of the Greenwheat plain, where the fields run out toward the fire mountains. In the spring of 1673 a pack of flame-mane wolves came down out of the volcano belt at night. They hunt the way they always hunt: they set the grass alight around their prey and drive it into the ring. They lit the young wheat around the farmstead. Fia's mother died that night. Her father ran with the baby through the burning wheat to the river. The Greenwheat was in spring flood, and the pack was behind him, and he could not swim it with a baby in his arms. He bound her to his javelin with willow bark and called on the wind. "Luluvie, if she lands alive she's yours, not mine." He threw. She landed in the green wheat on the far bank, crying. He went into the flood after her, and the wolves stopped at the water.

He kept the vow as he understood it. A child given to the wind does not sleep under a roof. He raised her in the open on the plain's edge, in a lean-to of straw by the river, on the milk of a mare that had lost its foal. At five she brought down hares with a sling. At eight she had a short bow. At twelve she ran across a standing field and the ears did not bend; the farmers swore to it. Wherever she ran, the wind was at her back.

He never called her his daughter. He called her Wind's. "Wind's, eat." "Wind's, the river's rising." Mothers on the plain wanted her for their sons, and she said whoever outran her could have her. Nobody did.

In the autumn of 1690 the dragon burned Granary City and the fields around it. Fia and her father came to the river beach under Twinbridge with everyone else and slept outside the walls, because she would not go inside them. She did not care about the gold on the bounty board. She cared that it had burned the wheat she ran on. Connor's band of hunters wanted her, because she was the fastest thing on the plain. She said no. "A band waits for its slowest man." They rode out with the lord's son and did not come back.

Oswin raced her for a ring, lost by thirty paces, and gave her the ring anyway. She said if she wore it she would sink, and hung it on her father's wrist. At night she sat outside the south gate and talked through the bars to the lord's hound, Ronan, who could not leave the gate. She would not come in; he would not come out. "Your lord won't tell you to go, so you don't go?" "Your father won't call you his, so you don't come inside?" The poets later made something of those nights. Neither of them ever admitted anything.

In the summer of 1691 she went after the dragon alone. All summer she ran in its shadow across the burnt plain; the fire could not catch the wind. One day it rose from a burning village with its wings low, and she ran in under it and put an arrow through the membrane of its left wing. Its blood came down on the black stubble, dark and hissing. By the end of the week both cities still standing knew: it bleeds.

Because it bleeds, the lord of Twinbridge raised a third army that autumn, twenty-six strong. Fia did not go; she never hunted in a band. Twenty-five of them died. Myren came back, sat down by the gate and cut twenty-five notches. Fia sat beside him and counted them out loud. She counted them three times.

Her father died on the river beach that winter. At the end he took her hand. "You're mine," he said. "I'll go and talk to the wind."

She never hunted the dragon again. In 1692 she ran ahead of it instead. Wherever it turned, she was in the next village before it, shouting them north. In the summer she ran into Twinbridge half a day ahead of the fire, and the city had time to crowd onto the north bridge. She shouted at Ronan through the south gate to come. He said the lord had not told him to go. There were more villages. She ran on.

She lived to be nearly eighty. In the fifty years after the dragon she killed two lords of the wild, and the poets sang her as the one who drew first blood on the Burning, but the last stroke had been Myren's, and she never brought down a thing like it. She never slept under a roof. When she was old she came back to the edge of the red plain, where Hob had been planting his half-yield of wheat for sixty years, and ran down his rows one more time. The ears did not bend. At the end of the row she sat down and did not get up, and that was where Hob found her.

On the highland the Greenwheat families still say "draw first blood" for the one who proves a thing can be hurt. They say it two ways. When a young one wants to go first, the old ones say it as praise. Then they tell him about the twenty-five.

## Ronan, the hound of Twinbridge

"Has it got pups? Give me one. Till it's grown, I'm your hound."

Ronan's blood was gold. He was born in a village on the plain to a mother who never said who his father was, and he grew strong past any reason. When he cut himself, what came out was golden. In those years there was no name for that. The village was a little afraid of him.

At eleven he walked to Twinbridge to see the city, with a sling and a stick. He came to the south gate after dark, when it was shut, on the night of the lord's midsummer feast, when the lord's guard-beast ran loose in the gate passage. It was a copper-hide wolf the lord had bought as a cub from highland hunters, twelve years at the gate; the lord's son Edwin had learned to walk holding its ruff. It came at Ronan out of the dark. Blades strike sparks off a copper-hide wolf. Ronan put a sling-stone into its open mouth and broke its neck with his hands.

The lord came out with torches and stood over it a long time. Ronan said, "Has it got pups? Give me one. Till it's grown, I'm your hound." Lord Harold took him at his word. Ronan slept in the south gatehouse. The pup grew in three years; Ronan did not leave. The lord taught him the spear the way he taught his own son, the bridgehead spear of his house, which is for holding a narrow crossing against whatever comes over it, and Ronan and Edwin grew up like brothers. People in the city called him the lord's dog. First it was an insult. Then it was a title.

When Ronan fought, something in him woke. His face changed, he stopped feeling anything, and beyond the gate he was holding he did not know friend from foe. Only someone who knew his name could call him back, and few people in the city used it.

In the late autumn of 1690 Edwin rode out with six of his father's riders and Connor's band, the first army. Ronan wanted to go. The lord said, "You keep the gate. When he comes back, you open it for him." Ronan waited on the north gate for ten days. On the tenth he heard a horse on the north bridge, and opened the gate. It was Edwin's horse with no one on it, and the farmer Myren walking behind.

After that Ronan wanted to go after the dragon more than anything, and the lord would not let him. "You're what I've got left. You keep the gate." He never told him he could go. Ronan opened the gate for Myren twice more. Oswin offered him a ring to come up the mountain as a sworn man; Ronan said a hound keeps one gate, and Oswin gave him the ring anyway. Hob brought him bread at sunset, and he never refused food offered at a fire; that was his mother's rule. At night Fia sat outside the gate and talked to him through the bars.

In the summer of 1692 Fia ran into the city half a day ahead of the dragon, and four thousand people crowded onto the north bridge. The lord would not leave the south gatehouse, where the bounty board was, where he had read out his son's name. Ronan would not leave the lord's gate. Then the dragon came and coiled itself around the city with its chin on the south gatehouse, and began calling the people on the walls one by one. "Call me king."

Ronan climbed from the gatehouse roof onto its jaw and drove his spear into the corner of its mouth, and the hound in him woke. That was not his gift; nothing his size could hold a thing that size by a gift. It was the spear in the soft of its mouth and a grip that would not open. For the best part of a morning the dragon shook its head and could not get him off, and it did not once look at the north bridge. Lord Harold stood in the gatehouse door and shouted "Ronan! Go!", the first time he had used the name in years and the first time he had ever told him to go. The name brought him back to himself. He shouted down that the people weren't all across, and did not let go.

When the dragon finally threw him, he was burned through. He tied himself to the bounty post by the gate with his belt so that he would stay on his feet, and held his spear up. The dragon put its head down to him. "Call me king." He did not answer. It waited. A few people who had not got away in time were hiding in the reeds across the south bridge, and they watched gold run down the post. Then a crow came down out of the smoke and settled on his shoulder, and the dragon knew he was dead, and burned the city. By then the north bridge was empty.

The poets call him the Hound of Twinbridge. The Greenwheat people on the highland say someone is "waiting for the crow" when they are waiting for a man to give in who never will. The wolf he raised from the pup went over the north bridge with the last of the people, because he told it to, and went back up to the highland its kind had come from. The big copper-coated dogs on the highland farms are said to be its get.

## Two shorter pieces for the people entries

**Connor's band.** Ten hunters from the snow line in the north of the Blackstone Highland, where copper-hide wolves run in packs. To join, you were chased through a wood and had to come out with your braid unravelled by nothing and no dead twig broken under your foot; then you were buried to the waist with a shield and a hazel stick and nine men threw spears at you at once; then you had to pull a thorn out of your foot at a full run. Their one rule: whoever dies on a hunt, his kin take no vengeance for him. They came to Twinbridge knowing the gold on the board did not exist; they came for the biggest hunt anyone had ever heard of. The night before they rode out, they sat at a fire on the river beach and said what each would do with his share of the gold that did not exist, and every answer was more ridiculous than the last. Connor said he would buy the plain new wheat. Ten days later Myren cut seventeen notches.

**Harold, lord of Twinbridge.** He nailed the bounty to the board by the south gatehouse himself: five hundred gold coins, the estate west of the city, a knighthood. He did not lie about it. The gold was on Granary City's ledgers, and the ledgers had burned; the estate he would give if it had not burned too; the knighthood was his to give. His son went with the first army. Every morning after that he fed the boy's horse himself. He raised the second army and the third, and read every name aloud at the south gate before they left. The only thing he could never bring himself to say was "go" to Ronan, until the morning it was too late.
