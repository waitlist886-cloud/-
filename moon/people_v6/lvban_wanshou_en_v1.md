# The Fenyuan Generation — hero tales (English drafts)

These are working drafts. The card text is retold in Chinese from them, not translated line by line.

## Oswin of Grey Ridge, the King of the Empty Seat

"I will go down to the wheat country and cut the fire out of that dragon's heart, and set it in this crown. If I do not bring it back, I do not come back to this hall."

Grey Ridge was the smallest of the iron towns on the eastern rim of the Blackstone Highland, and it had the longest hall on the highland: stand at the door and you could not see the fire at the far end. Its crown was a ring of black iron with three seats. Two held beast-cores, cut by Oswin's grandfather and his father from beasts they had slain with their own hands. The third seat was empty, and it was his. That was the rule of Grey Ridge: a king's son sets his own kill into the crown, or he does not wear it.

From eight to fourteen he was fostered in Granary City, down on the Greenwheat plain. Grey Ridge sold iron for Greenwheat grain, and the two houses traded children to keep the bargain honest. He learned to read tally sticks, to judge wheat by the smell, to drink the thin Greenwheat ale, and for six years he called the lord of Granary City "Father."

At fifteen, the autumn after the thunder season, a thunder-mane wolf leader came down off the high pastures to drag cattle. Oswin stood on the wall in the dark. Other people saw nothing out there. He saw a light, the beast's core burning in its chest like a lamp in a window, three hundred paces off. He threw. At dawn they found the wolf in a ditch with his javelin through the light. No one in three generations of his family had killed so young. And he gave the core away the same morning, to the herdsman whose cows had been taken, because the man had lost more than Oswin had won. The third seat stayed empty. His father laughed until he coughed.

He grew into a generous king's son, generous past sense. A good king is measured by how freely he gives; Oswin gave his food to refugees, his arm-rings to anyone who told a good story, and his promises to everyone. He loved riddles. "The more I am wounded, the richer my master. On the night the fire comes, I die before him." The answer was a tally stick. Whoever guessed won an arm-ring; whoever failed drank a horn of ale. Most of his rings went that way.

In the autumn of 1690 a red dragon landed on the wall of Granary City and told the people to call it king. The lord of Granary called it king. It burned the city anyway, because, it said, there was fear in the word. The lord was the man Oswin had called Father.

That night in the long hall Oswin put his hand on the fire-blackened oath stone and made his boast, and before the cup went round he stripped three rings off his arms and gave them to the three men sitting farthest from the fire. A boast made at a feast still holds when the sun comes up.

At the gate in the morning stood his father's twelve old sworn men. The youngest was forty-three. The oldest, Wulf, had one eye and a white beard. They had sworn at his father's fire: where the lord's son goes, we go. Oswin told them to go home. They said his father had told them to follow him. He told them again. Wulf put a javelin in his hand and said, "Walk. We need to be off the mountain by dark."

That was what weighed on him. A sworn man does not leave the field alive if his lord dies on it. His boast was taking him to a dragon, and it was taking twelve old men with him.

In the spring of 1691 Greenwheat raised its second army and Oswin led the vanguard. When the dragon rested between breaths, the scale under its throat went dark, and Oswin saw the core burning behind it. He threw, and hit, and did not go through. The second army died to the last man. The twelve stood in a ring around his body and none of them stepped back. Wulf fell last, cursing the dragon in the Grey Ridge tongue.

Myren was there. On his tally stick, the notches for that day include thirteen cut side by side.

The third seat of the Grey Ridge crown was never filled. The kings after him wore the crown with its empty seat and said they were keeping it for him, and the poets called him the King of the Empty Seat. In the winter of 1693 the last of the Greenwheat people came up the highland with nothing, and Grey Ridge took them in, because the boy had died for them. His riddles outlived his town. Highland children were still asking each other about the tally stick a thousand years later, and nobody remembered whose riddle it was.

## Inga of Molten Heart, who broke three swords

"How old were you that year?"
"Seventeen."
"Nobody can get into the crater."
"I did."

Dwarf babies grab for a hammer first. Inga crushed the rail of her cradle. At five she snapped an ironwood bar, at twelve she ground raw ore to powder in her palm. Every smith in Molten Heart who forged for her watched the work fail: hilts bent in her grip, hammer-heads split. One old smith made her three blades. When the third broke in her hand, he put out his own forge in front of the whole street and said he would not take the work. In a city whose first law is that the forges never go out, a smith quenching his fire is a disgrace worse than death. After that no smith would make anything for her.

Molten Heart judges people by their work, not their blood. A dwarf is what he has made. Inga was the strongest person in the city and the only one who had never set a piece before the anvil. Pincers bent in her hands, blanks cracked. Only Dagny wanted her. Dagny, of the line that kept the first forge, was trying to make a metal that could kill a dragon, and she was famous for a temper harder than slag. When Inga was nine, Dagny took her to the bellows of the first forge, a bellows bigger than a house with a lever of solid black iron, and slapped the lever. "Wind, at least, you can't break."

For seven years Inga worked those bellows. Each stroke turned the fire one shade whiter. Every one of Dagny's eleven thousand attempts was fired by her breath.

Nobody believed what she said. She said she had not lost a wrestling match since she was nine; nobody believed it until no one would wrestle her on Oath-Hammer Day. She said the fire had to burn white into blue; only Dagny believed her. She said she could hear the stone moving inside the mountain at night; the city laughed for a year, until a level of the mine came down and she had cleared everyone out of it half a day before. Rolf, an apprentice the same year as her, had the sharpest tongue in the city and spent it on her, and was always the first to stand beside her afterwards.

She fought with her hands. What she feared was touching people. Dwarves greet each other by clasping forearms; she pressed her fist to her own chest instead. Strangers thought she was proud. Her friends knew she was afraid of breaking an arm.

She wanted two things: something she could not break, and a hand she could safely hold.

Dagny's metal lacked one last ingredient. Dagny never said what. Inga decided it was the bone of a true dragon. Above the city, at the top of the dead volcano, was the crater, forbidden ground; dwarves dig down, never up, and the old songs said there was a heap of dragon bones at the bottom.

In the winter of 1692 she was seventeen. She went up alone. When she came back she said she had jumped into the crater, wrestled all night with a golden, winged shadow, and woken with a bone in her hands. She ran down the mountain with it. She reached the first forge the morning after Dagny had thrown herself into the fire on the eleven-thousand-and-first attempt. She was one night late.

The bone became a sword. She never sharpened it and never drew it. Rolf said it in front of a full room: "The crater is forbidden, nobody can get in. And none of us has ever seen you draw that sword." She said, "Don't need to."

She lived three hundred years. With her bare hands she broke the jaw of a siege-beast at the gate of a highland mining town while the garrison watched from the wall. She dug seven people out of eleven from under a fallen rock layer. In three hundred years she never lost a wrestling match. On the day she died she drew the sword once, and the people around her saw that it really was dragon bone. Whether she ever went into the crater, nobody can prove either way. The sword is Unground Bone: dragon bone enough to hold a legend, still waiting for the deed. Dwarf children learned to talk back with her three words.

## Hob of Millstone City, the Snow Granary

"Kumironi, one more granary. Just one. Give it, and tomorrow morning I'll sweep your shrine. Three times. No, twice. I'll never get up for three."

Millstone City stood under the cliff at the east end of the Greenwheat plain, where the river came down off the rock and turned dozens of water mills. Hob's family had the seventh. He was the youngest at the mill and the sleepiest, flour in his hair, freckles on his face, short for fifteen, smelling of new flour wherever he went.

When he was seven, rust blight got into the family's wheat. He knelt at the edge of the field and asked Kumironi, the harvest goddess, to take it away, and in the morning it was gone. From then on, anything he asked for that fell within the harvest was answered. In those years nobody knew that gods walked among people. Prayers were answered sometimes and sometimes not. Hob's were answered every time, so the town called him the wheat-god's boy, half as a joke and half out of fear. The temple's chief priest could not decide whether he was a saint or a witch and made him kneel at the shrine every tenth day; he fell asleep kneeling every time. His mother said: don't ask so much, whatever you ask for has to be paid back.

The goddess dealt only in what grows in the field, what is stored in the granary and what is cooked in the pot. An empty granary could be filled once a night; a field could ripen half a month early; bread could rise without yeast; a cow that could not calve could be eased. Nothing else. He asked her once not to let a man die, and she did not answer. He asked her to stop a fire, and she did not answer. He cursed her and then went back to asking for wheat. She never answered anything he asked for himself: a dry day for a nap, a neighbour girl looking his way. "The god doesn't owe me," he said. "I owe her."

Along the field ridges, wherever he went, there was a gleaning girl, sunburnt, a wheat ear in her braid. Hob swore she was Kumironi. The girl laughed: "Have you ever seen a god?" Everyone else saw a poor girl picking up dropped ears.

When Granary City burned, the lord of Millstone sent Hob down to Twinbridge with the grain carts. Thousands of people from Granary were camped on the river beach below the south bridge. He knelt among the empty carts, made his offer, fell asleep on an empty sack, and in the morning the carts were full.

Everyone wanted him. The lord of Twinbridge wanted him with the army, because an army that eats is worth more than a hundred knights. The temple wanted him kept at the shrine. Oswin offered an arm-ring if he would come to Grey Ridge. Hob wanted to go home to the seventh mill, sleep on the flour sacks and listen to the millstone all day. He was afraid of fire; the smell of smoke sent him to the river. This war was against a fire dragon.

He haggled with everything: with the goddess, with people, with beasts. Once a wolf cornered him behind the mill and he talked wheat prices at it until it left. When someone came to him bleeding, he gave them bread first and then said, "She only does wheat. You're bleeding. Go to someone else." When someone called him a saint, he waved it off: "Don't. Saints have to get up early." If anyone in the camp had not eaten, he could not sleep.

Before the dragon came over Millstone City in 1693, he went home and led everyone who was left out through the waterway at the foot of the cliff behind the mill. All three cities were gone, the kingdom was gone, and forty-three thousand people climbed west onto the Blackstone Highland in the dead of winter. The crossing took ninety-seven days. Every empty granary on the way was full the next morning, and Ragna walked ahead of them listening for water under the snow. No one starved. Once he knelt on the red soil and asked for wheat, and for the first time in his life there was no answer. Grey Ridge took the Greenwheat people in and settled them in the valley under the ridge. The poets called him the Snow Granary, because the granaries filled even in snow, and the mining towns of the highland's eastern rim still break a loaf for passing strangers on midwinter day, to pay back that winter.

Legends do not grow old. For thousands of years afterwards he planted along the edge of the red plain, one row at a time. In the age of the kingdoms the villages on the edge of the red soil reaped half of what a field elsewhere would give, and the old people say that half was his asking.

## Ragna of the High Pastures, who heard water under stone

"Nobody called me. I came."

Ragna was a shepherd's daughter from the high pastures above Grey Ridge. She went barefoot all year, the soles of her feet hard as horn, and she knew every spring on the ridge. She could lay her ear against rock and hear water running underneath.

When she was six, an old shepherd with no sheep came over the pass. She gave him her whole lunch. He showed her how to put her ear to the stone. "There's more water under the rock than on top of it," he said, and went on over the next ridge. She saw him again every few years, always on the ridge, never any older.

When she was ten, a drought dried the high pastures. She walked until she heard water under a slab of grey rock, struck it with her crook, and a spring came out. The flock lived. Grey Ridge half believed her and called her the water-listener.

Before Oswin was sent down to the plain, they herded together. She taught him the sling; he taught her riddles. When he came back at fourteen he wore arm-rings and spoke like a prince. At fifteen, after the wolf, he gave her the first ring he had ever given anyone. She wore it on her ankle, because she was always barefoot.

In the autumn of 1690 the water under the ridge tasted of ash. She did not wait to be asked. She followed Oswin's band down to the plain, uninvited, because the water was telling her something was wrong down there, and because she had always wanted to follow the water to wherever it went. She had never seen the sea.

She fought with a sling and a crook, and ran down goats on bare feet. She called Oswin by the name she had called him when he was seven, which no one else was allowed to do, and she was the only person who was not impressed by his boasts. She knew his boast would kill him, and she could not drag him home.

The night before the second army marched, Oswin sent her back to the camp to find water for the morning and left her a riddle to answer when he came back. He did not come back. She carried his spear up the highland to Grey Ridge.

In the winter of 1693 the Greenwheat people came up onto the highland with nothing. For ninety-seven days she walked ahead of forty-three thousand people, listening for water under snow and stone; Hob kept the granaries full behind her. Grey Ridge gave them the valley under the ridge.

About 1706 the valley's water began to drop, until the bottoms of the wells showed stone. Something had coiled itself over the spring. What happened next is sung two ways, by the people on the ridge and the people in the valley, and the two sides have despised each other ever since. In the ridge song she went down because there were still people in the valley; she fought three days and three nights, set the water free, and walked out of the valley mouth with her head up. In the valley song she went down because the thing had something she wanted; it took her one night, she dug out its heart and took it with her, the water came back on its own, and she walked out of the valley mouth with her head down, walking fast. Both songs agree on this much: she went down alone, the thing died, the water came back, and after she walked out of the valley mouth no one ever saw her again. Her sword was found driven deep into the rock at the valley mouth. That is Valley-Mouth Scale. Neither song ever says her name.

## Cadoc of Underlid, who drove the cattle up the mountain

"Old Lid. Come home."

Underlid was a village at the foot of a volcano in the southern belt. For centuries a red dragon had lain across the mountain's mouth with its chin on the crater's rim, and the village believed that as long as it lay there the mountain would not wake. They called it Old Lid. Every autumn, in the month of falling leaves, they drove one ox and one cart of salt up the mountain, and Old Lid took them and did not come down.

Each generation one child drove the tribute up. On that child's first drive, the dragon let a drop of its blood fall on the child's tongue, so that it would know the child's smell. Cadoc drove the tribute for seven autumns, from nine to sixteen. Old Lid talked to him. It asked about the village and the taste of the salt. In a snowstorm once it let him sleep against its warm jaw.

Fire never burned him after that drop of blood. He also heard, from the first drive on, what every beast called itself in its own heart. Old Lid called itself Old Lid. It had taken the village's name for its own.

In the autumn of 1690 his cart broke a wheel on the mountain path and he was half a day late. When he reached the crater, Old Lid did not look at the ox. It was looking north-east. It said that the seven kings were lying down, and a dragon ought to have a king. Cadoc listened for its name and heard a different one now. It called itself King. It flew. That night the mountain woke, and Underlid lay under the lava by morning. Cadoc was still on the path with the ox and the salt.

He dragged them north-east for forty days, following the smoke, and reached the river beach at Twinbridge among the people of Granary City. The hunters there laughed at a boy who meant to pay tribute to a dragon. "The bargain was made in autumn," he said. "Autumn isn't over." Hob asked him for the ox to feed the camp. He said it belonged to Old Lid. In the winter a child in the camp could not get warm and would not eat, and Cadoc led the ox to Hob himself.

He believed that if it heard its old name it would remember, and come home. Oswin wanted to cut out its heart for his crown; the night before the second army marched, the two of them shouted at each other across the fire. Cadoc did not march with the second army.

In the autumn of 1692 the fourth army formed up at the edge of the red soil below the cliff. At dawn Cadoc walked out ahead of the line, dragging the cart of salt, and stood on the red line and called it. "Old Lid. Come home." The whole army saw it: the great head came down toward him, and stopped, for one breath, perhaps two. Then the dragon said, "Call me king." Its fire was half red and half black. The red did not burn him. The black did. The fourth army died there to the last man except Myren, and the first notch Myren cut for that day was for Cadoc.

Where he stood, the salt from the cart burned into the soil. There is a white line a few paces long across the red plain at the foot of the cliff, and rain has never washed it out. On the highland, the old Greenwheat families say "call it by its old name" when they mean giving someone one last chance to remember who they were.
