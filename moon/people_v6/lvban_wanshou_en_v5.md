# The Fenyuan Generation: the song and the hero tales (English drafts, fourth pass)

These are working drafts. The card text is retold in Chinese from them, not translated line by line. Where the Chinese and the English differ, the Chinese is the one that goes into the card.

Names used below: Grey Ridge (灰岭), the Blackstone Highland (黑石高地), the Thousand-Ear Plain (千穗原) of the kingdom of Thousand Ears (千穗国), Eartower (穗塔城), Twinarch (双虹城), Wheelsong (鸣轮城), the Ear River (穗河), Molten Heart (熔铁炉心), the Dragonbone Heap (龙骨堆), Mountainsleep village (眠山村), the Mountain-sleeper (眠山), Myren (麦伦), Dagny (达格妮), Kumironi (库米罗妮, harvest), Luluvie (璐璐薇, wind). The dragon is Fenyuan, the Burning, later sung as the Crownless Dragon (无冠之龙). Event names: the Night the Mountain Woke (山醒之夜), One Word: King (一声王), the Empty Saddle (空鞍), the Last Ring (最后一环), the Arrow from the Shadow (影下一箭), Following the Blood (追血), the Crow's Landing (鸦落), the Morning of the White Line (白线之晨), Blue Fire (蓝火), Three Days on the Cliff (崖上三日), the Winter of Nine Gates (九门之冬); the whole is the Burning of Three Cities (三城之焚).

## The Burning of Three Cities (the song, English draft)

The Mountain-sleeper who held the fire down, the Crownless Dragon, Fenyuan.

In the month of Falling Leaves in the year 1690 of the Moonsilver Age it stood up from the crater it had lain across for centuries and flew northeast. It wanted the three cities of the Thousand-Ear Plain to call it king. In three years nobody said it right.

### The Night the Mountain Woke

On a volcano in the southern fire-mountains a red dragon lay across the crater with its chin on the rim. The village below believed that while it lay there the mountain slept, so they called it the Mountain-sleeper, and the village Mountainsleep. Every autumn, in the month of Falling Leaves, the tithe-bearing family sent a child up with an ox and a cart of salt, and for a year the dragon did not come down.

That autumn it heard words the village could not hear. "The seven kings are all lying down. Dragons should have a king. Why not you?" The seven kings were the dragon kings who bowed to heaven at the end of the Burial of Fire.

That year the child was Cadoc. His cart lost a wheel and he came half a day late. The Mountain-sleeper did not look at the ox. It was looking northeast, and when Cadoc listened for the name it called itself, the name was no longer the Mountain-sleeper. It was King. It spread its wings and went, and the crater under it lay open, red as an oven with the door torn off.

That night the mountain woke.

The other fire-mountain villages watched the glow all night from far off; the Night the Mountain Woke is their name for it. In the morning there was a red river where Mountainsleep had been, and on the mountain road a boy, an ox and a cart of salt.

### One Word: King

Thirty li ahead of it the well water went warm. Ten li ahead of it the wheat ears blackened from the tip. Half a day ahead of it the cattle broke their tethers and ran north. Then it came down on the wall of Eartower, the royal city of the Thousand Ears, where the granaries stood taller than the walls, hooked its forefeet over the battlements and spread its wings, and half the sky was red.

"Call me king."

The lord of Eartower called it king. It burned the city anyway; there was no fear in the word, it said. The granaries burned for seven days, and wherever the wind went it smelled of scorched bread, as far as Twinarch, three or four days off. Every tally stick in the kingdom was firewood by morning. The Eartower folk on the river flats at Twinarch never said more about that night than "one word: king." In Twinarch Lord Harold nailed a bounty beside the south gatehouse: five hundred gold, an estate west of the city, a knight's name. The gold was written in the ledgers of Eartower, the ledgers had burned, and he told everyone so.

### That Autumn

That autumn none of them knew each other's names.

Oswin, the young king of Grey Ridge, came down off the highland; his father's black iron crown still had an empty seat, waiting for him, and behind him came twelve round shields painted with a mountain.

Inga the dwarf girl leaned on the bellows of the First Forge in Molten Heart, a bar three grown men could barely move.

Hob from the seventh mill of Wheelsong slept on an empty sack among empty grain carts.

Ragna, a barefoot shepherd girl of Grey Ridge, tasted ash in the water under the ridge and followed without being asked.

Cadoc, the last tithe-bearer of Mountainsleep, walked forty days northeast after the smoke with an ox and a cart of salt.

Fia, who had been given to the wind, slept outside the walls of Twinarch, because she did not go where the wind could not.

Ronan, the Lord's Hound, slept in the south gatehouse with his spear by his hand, and his blood was gold.

And there was a wheat farmer named Myren. He had been out in the fields the night his city burned and came back to find only the gate. He sat outside Twinarch with half a tally stick and a knife.

### The Empty Saddle

In the winter of 1690 the first army went out of the north gate, eighteen of them: Connor's ten hunters down from the snow line of the highland, Lord Harold's son Edwin with six riders, and Myren walking behind. Ronan watched from the north gatehouse for ten days. On the tenth he heard hooves on the north bridge and opened the gate. Edwin's horse came in.

There was no one in the saddle.

Myren walked in behind it, sat down by the gate, cut seventeen notches in his half tally stick, one for each, and went to sleep. From the next day Harold fed the empty-saddled horse himself every morning and would not let the grooms touch it.

### The Last Ring

In the spring of 1691 the second army went out, forty-two, and in front walked Oswin and his twelve old sworn men. Oswin gave past all sense, rings to whoever spoke well, bread to whoever was running, his word to everyone, and what he swore at a feast he did at dawn. When the dragon had breathed and was resting, the scale under its throat went dark, and he saw the core burning behind that scale and threw. It struck. The javelin stood in the dark scale, and the light behind it went on burning.

It finished resting.

The next fire fell where he stood. The twelve closed around his body shield to shield. The oath they had sworn in the long hall long ago, they kept that day word for word: "Where my lord falls, I stand and do not retreat, to the last man." The last to fall was one-eyed Wulf, who shouted at the dragon in the tongue of Grey Ridge, "You have no crown. We have a king." When the fire passed, twelve round shields painted with the mountain of Grey Ridge still stood in a ring. Myren walked back alone and cut forty-one notches, thirteen side by side. Wulf's words came back with him, the poets sang them, and from then on the dragon was also the Crownless. The blind old singer in the long hall of Grey Ridge called that fight the Last Ring. The last ring the ring-giver gave away, he sang, was twelve old men standing around him.

### The Arrow from the Shadow

All the summer of 1691 Fia ran alone in the dragon's shadow over the burned stubble. It never looked back for the small thing running under it. One day it rose from a burning village with its wings low and the fire behind it, and the fire shone through its left wing so that she could see every vein in the membrane and where it was thinnest. She ran in under it and put an arrow through. No blood came. What came was black, and it smoked where it dripped on the black stubble. In seven days both remaining cities were saying it.

"It can be hurt."

On the strength of that arrow Harold raised a third army that autumn, twenty-six, and Wheelsong never called that army anything but Following the Blood, and did not mean it as praise. Twenty-five died under the cliff. Myren walked back and cut twenty-five notches, and Fia sat beside him and counted them aloud, three times.

### The Crow's Landing

In the summer of 1692 the wells of Twinarch went warm. Fia reached the walls half a day ahead of the dragon, went inside a city wall for the first time in her life, and ran shouting from the south gate to the north, and four thousand people crowded onto the north bridge. Harold would not leave the south gatehouse, where his bounty board hung with three lists of names beside it, and Ronan would not leave his lord's gate.

The dragon came. It coiled around Twinarch as if the city were its throne, laid its chin on the south gatehouse and called the people on the walls one by one. "Call me king." Ronan leapt from the gatehouse roof onto its jaw and drove his spear into the corner of its mouth, and the dog in him woke. For half the morning it shook its head and could not throw him, and never once looked at the north bridge. Harold stood in the gatehouse door and shouted, "Ronan, go." It was the first time in years he had said the name, and the first time he had told him to go. The name brought Ronan back to himself. He shouted down, "They're not all across." He did not let go.

When it threw him at last he was burned through. He belted himself to the bounty post so he could stand, spear still raised. The dragon lowered its head to him. "Call me king."

He did not answer.

It waited. A few people hiding in the reeds by the south bridge saw golden blood running down the post. Then a crow dropped out of the smoke onto his shoulder, and only then did the dragon know he was dead, and burn the city. By then the north bridge was empty, and Harold died in the gatehouse. The people from the reeds reached Wheelsong, and Wheelsong's poet, when he had heard them out, sang that day as the Crow's Landing and Ronan as the Hound of Twinarch. Of the four thousand on the north bridge, not one saw how he died.

### The Morning of the White Line

In the autumn of 1692, the month of Falling Leaves again, the lord of Wheelsong raised a fourth army under the cliff, thirty-two. The red earth around the dragon's nest had spread from the clifftop down to the cliff foot. At dawn Cadoc walked out of the line hauling the salt cart himself. The ox had gone to Hob back in the first winter, the year a child on the river flats was too cold to eat. The month of Falling Leaves was the month of the tithe. The bargain was made in autumn, and autumn was not over. He walked to the red line alone and called, "Mountain-sleeper. Come home."

The whole army saw the great head come down until its breath stirred his hair, and stop, for a breath, maybe two. Then the dragon said, "Call me king." Its fire was half red and half black. The red could not burn him. The black could.

Myren walked back alone and cut thirty-one notches, the first one for Cadoc. When they asked him how it had gone he said one word. "Salt." Where Cadoc stood the salt burned into the ground, and across the red earth under the cliff there is a white line a few paces long that has not faded by the Age of Strife. When Wheelsong's poet sings that day, he begins with the white line.

### Blue Fire

That winter Inga went alone to the Dragonbone Heap three days north of the forge city. For eight years she had worked the bellows of the First Forge; every fire of the First Forge smith's thousands of tries was hers. Years before, she had said the fire had to burn white into blue before it was right; no one in the city believed her except that smith. She said afterwards that she jumped into the crater and wrestled all night with a shadow, a shadow all gold light with two wings spread, and woke with a bone in her hand. She carried it back as fast as she could. On the last night, coming over the last ridge, she saw the top of the forge-mountain glowing blue, the fire gone past white into blue. She ran the rest of the night and reached the First Forge at dawn. The smith had gone into it the night before. It was the eleven thousand and first try.

Inga was a night late.

From that day the forge was the Mother Forge. On the day the sword was forged from that pour, it was Inga at the bellows. Since then dwarves do not say "smelt" at the forge. They say, "Ask her to come out," and they mean the smith in the fire.

### Three Days on the Cliff

In 1693 the dragon came over Wheelsong, the last city. The river that falls off the cliff and turns the mills of Wheelsong turned to steam halfway down. Hob led the people who were left out by the water channel behind the mills, along the foot of the cliff.

The fifth time there was no army. Myren took the sword from the Mother Forge and climbed the cliff. He was afraid of heights, and had never in his life been higher than a city wall.

He fought it three days and two nights. By the third day black was seeping from its wounds, dripping onto the clifftop rocks and smoking. It rose high with Myren clinging to its back and lit a ring of fire on its own head.

The Crownless Dragon gave itself a crown.

Every fire on the plain leaned toward it like grass in a wind. The people who had escaped by the mill channel looked back from far away and could still see a ring of fire hanging over the cliff. Myren's sword broke. He walked along the dragon for half a day until he was under its jaw, and drove the broken blade in to the hilt. The crown went out. Man and dragon fell out of the sky onto the cliff edge and over it. They never found him. The blade was stuck in a crack at the foot of the cliff. All three cities were gone, the earth was red several feet down, and from then on the Thousand-Ear Plain was the Red Plain.

### The Winter of Nine Gates

In the winter of 1693 the forty-three thousand left of the Thousand Ears climbed onto the Blackstone Highland. The first night snow buried the road to the pass. Ragna lay on the snow half the night and heard a stream running under it, coming down from the pass, and at dawn she led them up along it. At the top, in the snow, there was a hollow where someone had sat, and a few holes from a staff. She looked at it and said nothing.

Then they walked the eastern rim of the highland from one iron town to the next. No town could hold forty thousand, and none dared open to forty thousand strangers. Nine towns, nine gates, and on nine walls men stood with torches looking down. Every morning every empty grain cart was full, and Hob slept beside them and could not be woken. Ragna walked in front, barefoot on the snow, listening under snow and stone for water, and forty-three thousand walked in her footprints. In ninety-seven days no one starved and no one died of thirst.

On the ninety-seventh day Grey Ridge opened its gate.

Grey Ridge was the smallest town on the rim, and the last. When the grain carts rolled in under the gate there was still snow on them, and a Grey Ridge child on the wall shouted "Snow Granary," and the name stuck. The Thousand-Ear folk had counted: nine gates shut, the tenth opened, and ever since they have called any house that takes in people with nowhere to go a tenth gate. The one who opened it that day was the old king of Grey Ridge, Oswin's father. He wore the black iron crown with the third seat empty, pushed the gate open with his own hands, and said his son had died for the Thousand-Ear people.

### What Was Left

Around 1720 Inga left the forge city alone to open a deep seam at Grey Ridge, tore apart a fallen seam with her bare hands and dug seven of eleven miners out alive, and broke the jaw of a siege-beast at the gate while the whole town watched. Her bone sword never left its sheath in her life. Around 1860 Fia, old by then, came back to the edge of the Red Plain and ran once more over the rows Hob had sown there, and not one ear bent; at the end of the row she sat down and did not get up. Hob sowed the edge of the Red Plain for thousands of years, and the edge yields half of what other land does; the old people say that half was prayed out of it one row at a time.

The earth of the Red Plain is red to this day, with a white line across it under the cliff. The Mother Forge still burns, and there is a shallow hollow in the shape of a person in its wall. The big copper-coated dogs in Thousand-Ear houses on the highland are said to come from the copper wolf Ronan raised from a pup. Before it died the dragon gave itself a crown of fire, and when the fire went out the crown was gone. Oswin had sworn, "If I do not bring it back, I do not return to this hall, I do not sit in this seat." The crown of Grey Ridge has had an empty seat for thousands of years, and it is still there.

When Thousand-Ear children on the highland play at hopscotch they count Myren's tally stick: empty saddle, seventeen. Stood in a ring, forty-one. Chased black blood, twenty-five. A cart of salt, thirty-one. Fifth time, nobody carved.

### Calling the Names

When a singer finishes the song, he calls the names one by one, and the room says each one after him.

The young king of Grey Ridge, the Last Ring, King of the Empty Seat.

Forge-breath of the First Forge, the Jaw at the Gate, Inga Barehand.

The Seventh Mill, Ninety-Seven Days, Snow Granary.

Grey Ridge's Spring-listener, the Stream Under the Snow, Ragna.

The last tithe-bearer, the Morning of the White Line, the boy who called the dragon home.

Daughter given to the wind, the Arrow from the Shadow, Wind Before the Fire.

Gold-blooded keeper of the gate, the Crow's Landing, the Hound of Twinarch.

When he comes to Myren the singer calls nothing, and the room finishes for him with the last line of the counting song.

This is how the Thousand-Ear folk tell it. Grey Ridge's blind singer began it with the arm-rings. Wheelsong's song begins with the mills, and the song of Molten Heart has nothing in it but a forge. Later the taverns had a version in which seven went up the cliff with Myren and only he came down, and which seven depends on who is singing. The murals in the royal halls show Myren alone.

### How the sayings are used

These came down from those years, and the Thousand-Ear folk and Grey Ridge folk on the highland were still saying them long after. Each is said straight, said halfway for someone else to finish, and said as a joke.

The counting song: children skip to it, and whoever lands on "fifth time, nobody carved" is out. One calls a number and the others answer with the thing: "twenty-five" gets "chased black blood." Grown-ups use the last line as a joke: someone who did a good deed with no one watching is told "fifth time, nobody carved," and so is someone who tells everyone what he did.

"Call me king" was the dragon's. Children playing at dragons: the dragon shouts it, the Grey Ridge side answers "you have no crown." Grown-ups say someone giving himself airs is calling-me-king again. Grey Ridge men facing a bully still say Wulf's line, one saying "you have no crown" and the others finishing "we have a king."

"It can be hurt" spread after Fia's arrow; whoever first proves a hard thing can be beaten gets praised with it. The old answer "twenty-five," meaning think of the twenty-five first. A boy who throws his big brother for the first time jumps up shouting "it can be hurt," and his grandfather answers as always, "Twenty-five."

"Call it once by its old name" means give a changed man one last chance to know himself, and the usual answer is Cadoc's own line, "autumn isn't over," meaning there is still time. When an old friend becomes a foreman and starts putting on airs, his mates call him by his boyhood nickname in front of everyone and say they are calling him by his old name.

"Waiting for the crow" is waiting for someone who will never give in to give in. Two children in a staring match, and an impatient onlooker says they are waiting for the crow.

"The tenth gate" is how the Thousand-Ear folk say thank you. Every year on the day Grey Ridge opened its gate, each house breaks a loaf for passers-by; one says "nine gates shut," and the whole room answers "the tenth opened." A child home late and locked out bangs on the door shouting "where's the tenth gate?"

"What is said at a feast still holds at dawn" is the old highland saying for oaths, Oswin's favourite. When someone swears at a feast in the long hall, the others pin it down: one says "what is said at a feast," the room answers "still holds at dawn." Whoever promised a neighbour, drunk, to fix his roof finds the neighbour at his door with a ladder in the morning, saying the same. Grey Ridge folk part with "keep the fire in" like everyone else, and add "the mountain of Grey Ridge above us"; that came down from the twelve shields.

## Oswin, the young king of Grey Ridge

"By the fire of Grey Ridge and the crown of my fathers I swear it. I go down to the wheat country and take the fire out of that dragon's heart and set it in the third seat of this crown. If I do not bring it, I do not come back to this hall, I do not sit in this chair, I do not drink one drop of its ale."

Grey Ridge was the smallest of the iron towns on the eastern rim of the Blackstone Highland, a day's climb above the Thousand-Ear Plain, and it had the longest hall on the highland: stand at the door and you could not see the fire at the far end. Its crown was a ring of black iron with three seats. Two held beast-cores, cut by Oswin's grandfather and his father from beasts they had slain with their own hands. The third seat was empty, and it was his. That was the rule of Grey Ridge: a king's son sets his own kill into the crown, or he does not wear it.

From eight to fourteen he was fostered in Eartower. Grey Ridge sold iron for Thousand-Ear grain, and the two houses traded children to keep the bargain honest. He learned to read tally sticks, to judge wheat by the smell, to drink the thin Thousand-Ear ale, and for six years he called the lord of Eartower "Father."

At fifteen, the autumn after the thunder season, the leader of a thunder-mane wolf pack came down off the high pastures to drag cattle. Oswin stood on the wall in the dark. Other people saw nothing out there. He saw a light, the beast's core burning in its chest like a lamp in a window, three hundred paces off. He threw. At dawn they found the wolf in a ditch with his javelin through the light. No one in three generations of his family had killed so young. He gave the core away the same morning, to the herdsman whose cows had been taken, because the man had lost more than Oswin had won. The third seat stayed empty. His father laughed until he coughed.

He grew into a generous king's son, generous past sense. On the highland a king is measured by how freely he gives; Oswin gave his food to refugees, his arm-rings to anyone who told a good story, and his promises to everyone. He loved riddles. "The more I am wounded, the richer my master. On the night the fire comes, I die before him." The answer was a tally stick. Whoever guessed won an arm-ring; whoever failed drank a horn of ale. Most of his rings went that way.

In the autumn of 1690 a red dragon landed on the wall of Eartower and told the people to call it king. The lord of Eartower called it king. It burned the city anyway, because, it said, there was no fear in the word. The lord was the man Oswin had called Father. That night in the long hall Oswin put his hand on the fire-blackened oath stone and swore, and before the cup went round he stripped three rings off his arms and gave them to the three men sitting farthest from the fire. A boast made at a feast still holds when the sun comes up.

At the gate in the morning stood his father's twelve old sworn men. The youngest was forty-three. The oldest, Wulf, had one eye and a white beard. They had sworn at his father's fire: where the lord's son goes, we go. Oswin told them to go home. They said his father had told them to follow him. He told them again. Wulf put a javelin in his hand. "Walk. We need to be off the mountain by dark."

That was what weighed on him. A sworn man does not leave the field alive if his lord dies on it. His oath was taking him to a dragon, and it was taking twelve old men with him. He said he was going for the empty seat. The old men knew he was going for the man he had called Father.

On the riverbank under Twinarch he wanted to give everyone something. He offered Hob a ring to come up to Grey Ridge; Hob would not. He shouted at Cadoc all night across a fire, one of them wanting to cut out the dragon's heart, the other wanting to call it home, and then gave Cadoc a ring, which Cadoc would not take. He raced Fia along the riverbank for a ring and lost by thirty paces. He offered the lord's hound Ronan a ring to come up the mountain as a sworn man; Ronan said a hound keeps one gate, and Oswin gave him the ring anyway. "I give it because you're worth it."

In the spring of 1691 the second army went out, forty-two strong, and Oswin and his twelve led it. When the dragon rested between breaths, the scale under its throat went dark, and Oswin saw the core burning behind it. He threw, and hit; the javelin stood in the dark scale and the light behind it went on burning. The dragon finished its breath. The next fire came down where he stood. The twelve stood in a ring around his body, as they had sworn in the long hall, and none of them stepped back. Wulf fell last. He shouted at the dragon in the Grey Ridge tongue: "You have no crown. We have a king." When the fire passed, the twelve shields still stood in a ring. Myren came back, the only one. Among the forty-one notches he cut that day, thirteen sit side by side. Myren brought Wulf's words home with him, and the poets made a name out of them: the Crownless Dragon.

The third seat of the Grey Ridge crown was never filled. The kings after him wore the crown with its empty seat and said they were keeping it for him. The blind old singer of the long hall was the first to call him the King of the Empty Seat, and the highland took it up; he named that fight the Last Ring. In the winter of 1693 the last of the Thousand-Ear people came up the highland with nothing, and nine iron towns shut nine gates on them. On the ninety-seventh day they reached Grey Ridge, and his father, wearing the crown with the third seat empty, pushed the gate open himself and said his son had died for these people. Grey Ridge gave them every roof and fire it could spare, and when the highland snow melted it showed them the empty mining towns to the north. Grey Ridge itself held on another eighty-some years. Somewhere in those years, around 1720, a deep gallery under the town fell in and a siege-beast came battering at the gate, and the one who dug the miners out and broke the beast's jaw was a dwarf girl who had come out of Molten Heart alone to open its deep seams. His riddles outlived his town. Highland children were still asking each other about the tally stick thousands of years later, and nobody remembered whose riddle it was.

## Inga, Forge-breath of the First Forge

"How old were you that year?" "Seventeen." "Nobody can get into the crater." "I did."

Dwarf babies grab for a hammer first. Inga crushed the rail of her cradle. By her early teens she had snapped an ironwood bar in her grip and ground raw ore to powder in her palm. Every smith in Molten Heart who forged for her watched the work fail: hilts bent in her grip, hammer-heads split. One old smith made her three blades. When the third broke in her hand, he put out his own forge in front of the whole street and said he would not take the work. In a city whose first law is that the forges never go out, a smith quenching his fire is a disgrace worse than death. After that no smith would make anything for her, and behind her back the city called her the forge-quencher.

Molten Heart respects craft. A dwarf is what he has made. Inga was the strongest person in the city and the only one who had never set a piece before the anvil. Only Dagny wanted her. Dagny, of the line that kept the first forge, was trying to make a metal that could kill a dragon, and she was famous for a temper harder than slag. When Inga was nine, Dagny took her to the bellows of the first forge, a bellows bigger than a house with a lever of solid black iron that three grown dwarves could barely move, and slapped the lever. "Wind, at least, you can't break."

For six years Inga worked those bellows. Each stroke turned the fire one shade whiter. Every attempt Dagny made in those six years was fired by her, and to her face the city called her the Forge-breath of the First Forge, the breath her bellows gave it; in their tongue the word sounds just like forge-out, so it was courtesy to her face and a laugh behind her back.

Nobody believed what she said. She said she had not lost a wrestling bout since she was nine; nobody believed it until no one would wrestle her before Doan's Anvil on Oath-Hammer Day. She said the fire had to burn white into blue; only Dagny believed her. She said she could hear the stone moving inside the mountain at night; the city laughed for a year, until a level of the mine came down and she had cleared everyone out of it half a day before. Rolf, who was born the same year on the same street and had the sharpest tongue in the city, spent it on her, and was always the first to stand beside her afterwards.

She fought with her hands. What she feared was touching people. Dwarves greet each other by clasping forearms; she pressed her fist to her own chest instead. Strangers thought she was proud. Her friends knew she was afraid of breaking an arm. She wanted two things: something she could not break, and a hand she could safely hold.

Dagny's metal lacked one last ingredient. Dagny never said what. Inga decided it was the bone of a true dragon. Three days north of the city, in the mountains, there is a crater the old songs call the Dragonbone Heap, and they say the bottom of it is bones. The air that comes up out of it would soften iron. It is forbidden ground; dwarves dig down, never up.

In the winter of 1692 into 1693 she was seventeen. She went up alone. When she came back she said she had jumped into the crater and wrestled all night with a shadow, all gold light with two wings spread, and woken with a bone in her hands. She carried it back as fast as she could. On the last night, from the last ridge, she saw the top of the forge-mountain glowing blue, the fire white past white into blue, the way she had always said it must go. She ran the rest of the night and reached the first forge the morning after Dagny had thrown herself into the fire on the eleven-thousand-and-first attempt. She was one night late. From that day the first forge was called the Mother Forge, and the city called that night Blue Fire. On the day the sword for Myren was forged from that pour, it was Inga at the bellows.

The bone became a sword. She never sharpened it. Years later Rolf said it in front of a full room: "The crater is forbidden, nobody can get in." "I got in." "And none of us has ever seen you draw that sword." "Don't need to."

She lived three hundred years more, and reached the rank of hero, never legend. Around 1720 Grey Ridge, up on the Blackstone Highland, sent to the forge city's mountain gate for dwarves to open a new deep gallery; no one in the forge city would leave the mountain, and she went alone. That year the gallery fell in. She heard it coming half a day ahead and told everyone to go up; the Grey Ridge miners did not all believe her, and eleven stayed below. When the rock came down she tore it apart with her hands and dug seven of the eleven out alive. That winter a siege-beast came battering at the gate of Grey Ridge, and she broke its jaw with her bare hands while the whole town watched, the garrison on the wall among them, and Grey Ridge called her Barehand from then on. In three hundred years she never lost a wrestling bout. On the day she died she drew the sword once, and the people around her saw that it really was dragon bone. Whether she ever went into the crater, nobody can prove either way. The sword is Unground Bone: true dragon bone, enough to carry a legend, but everything she was known for she did with her hands, and the deed was never set into it. Dwarf children learned to talk back with her three words, and the grown-ups scolded them, and then laughed.

## Hob, the wheat-god's darling

"Kumironi, one more granary. Just one. Give it, and tomorrow morning I'll sweep your shrine. Three times. No, twice. I'll never get up for three."

Wheelsong stood under the cliff at the north-east end of the Thousand-Ear Plain, where the river came down off the rock and turned dozens of water mills. Hob's family had the seventh. He was the youngest at the mill and the sleepiest, flour in his hair, freckles on his face, short for fifteen, smelling of new flour wherever he went.

When he was seven, rust got into the family's wheat. He knelt at the edge of the field and asked Kumironi, the harvest goddess, to take it away, and in the morning it was gone. From then on, anything he asked for that fell within the harvest was answered. In those years nobody knew that gods walked among people. Prayers were answered sometimes and sometimes not. Hob's were answered every time, so the town called him the wheat-god's darling, half as a joke and half out of fear. The temple's chief priest could not decide whether he was a saint or a witch and made him kneel at the shrine every tenth day; he fell asleep kneeling every time. His mother said: don't ask so much, whatever you ask for has to be paid back.

The goddess dealt only in what grows in the field, what is stored in the granary and what is cooked in the pot. An empty granary could be filled once a night; a field could ripen half a month early; bread could rise without yeast; a cow that could not calve could be eased. Nothing else. He asked her once not to let a man die, and she did not answer. He asked her to stop a fire, and she did not answer. He cursed her and then went back to asking for wheat. She never answered anything he asked for himself: a dry day for a nap, a neighbour girl looking his way. "The god doesn't owe me," he said. "I owe her."

Along the field ridges, wherever he went, there was a gleaning girl, sunburnt, a wheat ear in her braid. Hob swore she was Kumironi. The girl laughed: "Have you ever seen a god?" Everyone else saw a poor girl picking up dropped ears.

When Eartower burned, the lord of Wheelsong sent Hob down to Twinarch with the grain carts. Thousands of people from Eartower were camped on the river beach below the south bridge. He knelt among the empty carts, made his offer, lay down on an empty sack, and in the morning the carts were full.

Everyone wanted him. The lord of Twinarch wanted him with the army, because an army that eats is worth more than a hundred knights. The temple wanted him kept at the shrine. Oswin offered an arm-ring if he would come to Grey Ridge. Hob wanted to go home to the seventh mill, sleep on the flour sacks and listen to the millstone all day. He was afraid of fire; the smell of smoke sent him to the river. This war was against a fire dragon.

He haggled with everything: with the goddess, with people, with beasts. Once a wolf cornered him behind the mill and he talked wheat prices at it until it left. When someone came to him bleeding, he gave them bread first and then said, "She only does wheat. You're bleeding. Go to someone else." When someone called him a saint, he waved it off: "Don't. Saints have to get up early." If anyone in the camp had not eaten, he could not sleep. He took bread to the lord's hound at the south gate every evening, because the boy never refused food.

That winter on the river beach he had fed thousands of mouths one at a time, and by 1693 his asking could fill the carts of a whole people on the march. In 1693, before the dragon came over Wheelsong, he went home and led everyone who was left out through the waterway at the foot of the cliff behind the mill. All three cities were gone, the kingdom was gone. That winter, the day before the people went up onto the highland, he knelt on the red soil at the edge of the burnt plain and asked for wheat, and for the first time in his life there was no answer. Nothing would grow in what the dragon had burned, not even for her.

Forty-three thousand people climbed onto the Blackstone Highland in the dead of winter. No iron town had room for forty thousand people, and none dared let that many strangers in; one by one they shut their gates. The people walked along the rim from town to town for ninety-seven days, past nine towns and nine shut gates, with torches on the walls looking down. Every empty cart was full the next morning, and Ragna walked ahead of them listening for water under the snow. No one starved. On the ninety-seventh day Grey Ridge opened its gate. The carts came in with snow still on them, and a Grey Ridge child on the wall called out, "The Snow Granary." The name stuck. The poets later sang that wherever he walked, it snowed and the carts were full. The Thousand-Ear folk counted nine gates shut and the tenth opened, and called that winter the Winter of Nine Gates, and any house that takes in people with nowhere to go a tenth gate. They settled in the empty towns of the north, and their grandchildren's grandchildren still break a loaf for passing strangers every year on the day Grey Ridge opened its gate, to pay back that winter.

Legends do not grow old. For thousands of years afterwards he planted along the edge of the red plain, one row at a time. Nothing would grow in the red itself, but the edge had given nothing either, and in the age of the kingdoms the villages there reaped half of what a field elsewhere would give. The old people say that half was his asking, one row at a time. Around 1860 the old huntress Fia came back to the edge of the red plain and ran down his rows one last time, and it was Hob who found her at the end of the row.

## Ragna, the Spring-listener

"Nobody called me. I came."

Ragna was a shepherd's daughter from the high pastures above Grey Ridge. She went barefoot all year, the soles of her feet hard as horn, and she knew every spring on the ridge. She could lay her ear against rock and hear water running underneath.

When she was six, an old shepherd with no sheep came over the pass. She gave him her whole lunch. He watched her lying with her ear to a rock. "There's more water under the stone than on top of it," he said, and went on over the next ridge. She saw him again every few years, always on the ridge, never any older.

When she was ten, a drought dried the high pastures. She walked all day until she heard water under a slab of grey rock, struck it with her crook, and a spring came out. The flock lived. The spring never dried. Grey Ridge half believed her, but from that year, whenever a well ran low, someone climbed the ridge and shouted, "Spring-listener, come down a moment."

Before Oswin was sent down to the plain, they herded together. She taught him the sling; he taught her riddles. When he came back at fourteen he wore arm-rings and spoke like a prince, and she still called him Strawstalk. At fifteen, after the wolf, he gave her the first ring he had ever given anyone. She wore it on her ankle, because she was always barefoot.

In the autumn of 1690 the water under the ridge tasted of ash. Nobody called her. She followed Oswin's band down to the plain, because the water was telling her something was wrong down there, and because she had always wanted to follow the water to wherever it went. She had never seen the sea. On the plain she raced Fia twice: up a slope Ragna won, across the stubble Fia won. Ragna said soft ground was cheating.

The night before the second army marched, Oswin sent her back to the camp to find water for the morning and left her a riddle. "I have no feet and I'm always walking, no mouth and I'm always talking, and where I've been the grass is green." She had the answer before he finished: water. He said it didn't count, she could tell him when he got back. He did not come back. Long afterwards she understood that he had sent her for water that night so that she would not follow him. She carried his spear up the highland to Grey Ridge, and the long hall took down a sea-coloured sword that had hung on its wall for three generations, and gave it to her for bringing the spear home. She stayed at Grey Ridge two more years; in the autumn of 1693, when word came up that Wheelsong had burned too, she went back down and waited below the pass for the Thousand-Ear people.

In the winter of 1693 the Thousand-Ear people came up onto the highland with nothing. On the first night snow buried the pass. She lay on the snow half the night and heard a stream running under it, coming down from the pass, and at dawn she led them upstream along it. At the top, in the snow, was a hollow where someone had sat, with a few staff holes beside it and no sheep tracks; it had been there before they came. For ninety-seven days she walked barefoot ahead of forty-three thousand people, who walked in her footprints, listening for water under snow and stone; Hob kept the carts full behind her. No one died of thirst.

About 1706 the water in the valley under the ridge began to drop, until the bottoms of the wells showed stone. Something had coiled itself over the spring. What happened next is sung two ways, by the people on the ridge and the people in the valley, who had always despised each other and now had one more thing to fight about.

The ridge sings it like this. "You're going down?" "There are still people in the valley." The valley people never once climbed up to see her; in a drought they came up and shouted for her and went back down. She never waited to be called. When the water dropped, she simply went. She fought three days and three nights. The sword cracked halfway and did not break. She let the water back into the valley and walked out of the valley mouth with her head up, and never once looked back. "Was it worth it?" "The valley has water."

The valley sings it another way. "You're going down?" "That thing has something I want." The ridge people looked down on the valley, drought or no drought. When the water dropped she came down on her own; nobody called her, and she never asked whether anyone wanted her. It took her one night. The sword cracked halfway and did not break. She dug out the thing's heart and took it with her; the water came back on its own once the thing was dead. She walked out of the valley mouth with her head down, walking fast. "Was it worth it?" She did not answer.

Both songs agree on this much: she went down alone, the thing died, the water came back, and after she walked out of the valley mouth no one ever saw her again. Her sword was found driven deep into the rock at the valley mouth, with a scale set in its guard that had come off the thing. The ridge says it was a dragon. The valley says it was a very big fish. The ridge says she left the sword for the valley; the valley says she threw it away. That is Valley-Mouth Scale. Neither song ever says her name, and the Thousand-Ear song of those years does not sing the valley at all.

## Cadoc, the last tithe-bearer

"Mountain-sleeper. Come home."

Mountainsleep was a village at the foot of a volcano in the southern belt. For centuries a red dragon had lain across the mountain's mouth with its chin on the crater's rim, and the village believed that as long as it lay there the mountain slept. They called it the Mountain-sleeper. Every autumn, in the month of falling leaves, they led one ox and one cart of salt up the mountain as tithe, and the Mountain-sleeper took them and did not come down.

Each generation one child of Cadoc's family carried the tithe up. On that child's first drive, the dragon let a drop of its blood fall on the child's tongue, so that it would know the child's smell. Children born in a family that had tasted dragon blood for centuries did not burn; Cadoc, as a toddler, picked coals out of the hearth and played with them. He also heard, from birth, what every beast called itself in its own heart. The Mountain-sleeper called itself the Mountain-sleeper. It had taken the village's name for its own.

He carried the tithe for eight autumns, from nine to sixteen. The Mountain-sleeper talked to him. It asked about the village and the taste of the salt. In a snowstorm once it let him sleep against its jaw, and the scale was warm.

In the autumn of 1690 his cart broke a wheel on the mountain path and he was half a day late. When he reached the crater, the Mountain-sleeper did not look at the ox. It was looking north-east. It said that the seven kings were lying down, and a dragon ought to have a king. Cadoc listened for its name and heard a different one now. It called itself King. It flew, and the crater under it lay open, red as an oven with the door torn off. That night the mountain woke, and by morning there was a red river where Mountainsleep had been. The other fire-mountain villages called it the Night the Mountain Woke; Cadoc only ever said "the day the Mountain-sleeper left." Cadoc was still on the path with the ox and the salt.

He dragged them north-east for forty days, following the smoke, and reached the river beach at Twinarch among the people of Eartower. The hunters there laughed at a boy who meant to pay tribute to a dragon. "The bargain was made in autumn," he said. "Autumn isn't over." Hob asked him for the ox to feed the camp. He said it belonged to the Mountain-sleeper. In the winter a child in the camp could not get warm and would not eat, and Cadoc led the ox to Hob himself.

He believed that if it heard its old name it would remember, and come home. Oswin wanted to cut out its heart for his crown, and the two of them shouted at each other across a fire all night. Cadoc did not march with the second army. He said it hadn't heard its old name yet; nobody should cut out its heart before that.

Twinarch burned in the summer of 1692, and that autumn the lord of Wheelsong raised the fourth army below the cliff, where the red soil had spread down from the top. At dawn, in the month of falling leaves, the month of the tithe, Cadoc walked out ahead of the line hauling the cart of salt himself, since the ox had gone to Hob, stood on the red line and called it. "Mountain-sleeper. Come home." The whole army saw it: the great head came down until its breath stirred his hair, and stopped, for one breath, perhaps two. Then the dragon said, "Call me king." Its fire was half red and half black. The red did not burn him. The black did. Myren came back, the only one, and the first of the thirty-one notches he cut that day was for Cadoc. Asked how it had gone, he said one word: "Salt."

Where he stood, the salt from the cart burned into the soil. There is a white line a few paces long across the red plain at the foot of the cliff, and it has not faded. Wheelsong's poet named that day the Morning of the White Line and sang Cadoc as the boy who called the dragon home. On the highland, the old Thousand-Ear families say "call it by its old name" when they mean giving someone one last chance to remember who they were.

## Fia, daughter given to the wind

"Wind, if she lands alive, she's yours, not mine."

Fia's father hunted on the south-west edge of the Thousand-Ear Plain, where the fields run out toward the fire mountains. In the spring of 1673 a pack of flame-mane wolves came down out of the volcano belt at night. They hunt the way they always hunt: they set the grass alight around their prey and drive it into the ring. They lit the young wheat around the farmstead. Fia's mother died that night. Her father ran with the baby through the burning wheat to the river. The Ear River was in spring flood, and the pack was behind him, and he could not swim it with a baby in his arms. He bound her to his javelin with willow bark and called on the wind. "Luluvie, if she lands alive she's yours, not mine." He threw. She landed in the green wheat on the far bank, crying. He went into the flood after her, and the wolves stopped at the water.

He kept the vow as he understood it. A child given to the wind does not sleep under a roof. He raised her in the open on the plain's edge, in a lean-to of straw by the river, on the milk of a mare that had lost its foal. At five she brought down hares with a sling. At eight she had a short bow. At twelve she ran across a standing field and the ears did not bend; the farmers swore to it. Wherever she ran, the wind was at her back.

He never called her his daughter. He called her Wind's. "Wind's, eat." "Wind's, the river's rising." The plain called her the wind's girl, and when the temple priest wrote her name down he wrote Daughter Given to the Wind. Mothers on the plain wanted her for their sons, and she said whoever outran her could have her. Nobody did.

In the autumn of 1690 the dragon burned Eartower and the fields around it. Fia and her father came to the river beach under Twinarch with everyone else and slept outside the walls, because she would not go inside them. She did not care about the gold on the bounty board. She cared that it had burned the wheat she ran on. Connor's band of hunters wanted her, because she was the fastest thing on the plain. She said no. "A band waits for its slowest man." They rode out with the lord's son and did not come back.

Oswin raced her for a ring, lost by thirty paces, and gave her the ring anyway. She said if she wore it she would sink, and hung it on her father's wrist. At night she sat outside the south gate and talked through the bars to the lord's hound, Ronan, who could not leave the gate. She would not come in; he would not come out. "Your lord won't tell you to go, so you don't go?" "Your father won't call you his, so you don't come inside?" Neither talked the other round.

In the summer of 1691 she went after the dragon alone. All summer she ran in its shadow across the burnt plain, and it never thought the small thing under its shadow worth turning round for. One day it rose from a burning village with its wings low and the fire behind it, so that the left wing glowed red and she could see every vein and where it was thinnest, and she ran in under it and put an arrow through the membrane of its left wing, where there were no scales and the skin was far thinner than scale. No blood came out. What oozed from the wound was black, and it smoked where it dripped on the black stubble. By the end of the week both cities still standing were saying it: "It can be hurt."

Because it could be hurt, the lord of Twinarch raised a third army that autumn, twenty-six strong. Fia did not go; she never hunted in a band. Twenty-five of them died. Myren came back, sat down by the gate and cut twenty-five notches. Fia sat beside him and counted them out loud. She counted them three times. Wheelsong called that army Following the Blood. The arrow came to be called the Arrow from the Shadow, and "it can be hurt" stayed in the language; when someone first proves a hard thing can be beaten, people praise him with it, and the old answer "twenty-five."

Her father died on the river beach that winter. At the end he took her hand. "You're mine," he said. "I'll go and talk to the wind."

She never hunted the dragon again. In 1692 she ran ahead of it instead. Wherever it turned, she was in the next village before it, shouting them north. In the summer she reached Twinarch half a day ahead of the fire. Before she went in she shouted up at Ronan in the south gatehouse to come; he said Harold had no one else. Then she went inside a city wall for the first time in her life and ran shouting from the south gate to the north, and the city had time to crowd onto the north bridge. There were more villages. She ran on.

She lived nearly two hundred years and reached the rank of hero, never legend. After the dragon she killed two lords of the wild, and the poets sang her as the first one to wound the Burning, and, because she always ran ahead of it, as the Wind Before the Fire, but the last stroke had been Myren's, and she never brought down a thing like it. She never slept under a roof. Around 1860, old at last, she came back to the edge of the red plain, where Hob had been planting his half-yield of wheat for a hundred and sixty years, and ran down his rows one more time. The ears did not bend. At the end of the row she sat down and did not get up, and that was where Hob found her.

## Ronan, the gold-blooded gatekeeper

"Has it got pups? Give me one. Till it's grown, I'm your hound."

Ronan's blood was gold. He was born in a village on the plain to a mother who never said who his father was, and he grew strong past any reason. When he cut himself, what came out was golden. In those years there was no name for that. The village was a little afraid of him.

At eleven he walked to Twinarch to see the city, with a sling and a stick. He came to the south gate after dark, when it was shut, on the night of the lord's midsummer feast, when the lord's guard-beast ran loose in the gate passage. It was a copper-hide wolf the lord had bought as a cub from highland hunters, twelve years at the gate; the lord's son Edwin had learned to walk holding its ruff. It came at Ronan out of the dark. Blades strike sparks off a copper-hide wolf. Ronan put a sling-stone into its open mouth and broke its neck with his hands.

The lord came out with torches and stood over it a long time. Ronan said, "Has it got pups? Give me one. Till it's grown, I'm your hound." Lord Harold took him at his word. Ronan slept in the south gatehouse. The pup grew in three years and the debt was paid, and Ronan did not leave. He said staying was his own business. The lord taught him the spear the way he taught his own son, the bridgehead spear of his house, which is for holding a narrow crossing against whatever comes over it, and Ronan and Edwin grew up like brothers. People in the city called him the lord's dog. First it was an insult. Then it became a title, said a little more grandly: the Lord's Hound.

When Ronan fought, something in him woke. His face changed, he stopped feeling anything, and beyond the gate he was holding he did not know friend from foe. Only someone who knew his name could call him back, and few people in the city used it.

In the winter of 1690 Edwin rode out with six of his father's riders and Connor's band, the first army. Ronan wanted to go. The lord said, "You keep the gate. When he comes back, you open it for him." Ronan waited on the north gate for ten days. On the tenth he heard a horse on the north bridge, and opened the gate. It was Edwin's horse with no one on it, and the farmer Myren walking behind.

After that Ronan wanted to go after the dragon more than anything, and he did not go. Harold had no one else. Harold only ever said one thing on the matter: "You're what I've got left. You keep the gate." Ronan opened the gate for Myren twice more. Oswin offered him a ring to come up the mountain as a sworn man; Ronan said a hound keeps one gate, and Oswin gave him the ring anyway. Hob brought him bread at sunset, and he never refused food offered at a fire; that was his mother's rule. At night Fia sat outside the gate and talked to him through the bars.

In the summer of 1692 Fia reached the city half a day ahead of the dragon, came inside the walls for the first time, and ran shouting through it, and four thousand people crowded onto the north bridge. The lord would not leave the south gatehouse, where the bounty board was, where he had read out his son's name. Ronan would not leave the lord's gate. Then the dragon came and coiled itself around the city with its chin on the south gatehouse, and began calling the people on the walls one by one. "Call me king."

Ronan climbed from the gatehouse roof onto its jaw and drove his spear into the corner of its mouth, and the hound in him woke. With a spear in the soft of its mouth and two hands that would not open, he hung on for the best part of a morning; the dragon shook its head and could not get him off, and it did not once look at the north bridge. Lord Harold stood in the gatehouse door and shouted "Ronan! Go!", the first time he had used the name in years and the first time he had ever told him to go. The name brought him back to himself. He shouted down, "They're not all across," and did not let go.

When the dragon finally threw him, he was burned through. He tied himself to the bounty post by the gate with his belt so that he would stay on his feet, and held his spear up. The dragon put its head down to him. "Call me king." He did not answer. It waited. A few people who had not got away in time were hiding in the reeds across the south bridge, and they watched gold run down the post. Then a crow came down out of the smoke and settled on his shoulder, and the dragon knew he was dead, and burned the city. By then the north bridge was empty.

The people hiding in the reeds came to Wheelsong afterwards and told the story there, and a Wheelsong poet called that day the Crow's Landing and was the first to sing him as the Hound of Twinarch. The Thousand-Ear people on the highland say someone is "waiting for the crow" when they are waiting for a man to give in who never will. The wolf he raised from the pup went over the north bridge with the last of the people, because he told it to, and went back up to the highland its kind had come from. The big copper-coated dogs on the highland farms are said to be its get.

## Two shorter pieces for the people entries

**Connor's band, the ten wolves of the snow line.** Ten hunters from the snow line in the north of the Blackstone Highland, where copper-hide wolves run in packs. To join, you were chased through a wood and had to come out with your braid unravelled by nothing and no dead twig broken under your foot; then you were buried to the waist with a shield and a hazel stick and nine men threw spears at you at once; then you had to pull a thorn out of your foot at a full run. Their one rule, which all ten say together before a hunt: "Who dies by the hunt, his blood is not claimed." Whoever dies on a hunt, his kin take no vengeance for him. They came to Twinarch knowing the gold on the board did not exist; they came for the biggest hunt anyone had ever heard of. The night before they rode out, they sat at a fire on the river beach and said what each would do with his share of the gold that did not exist, and every answer was more ridiculous than the last. Connor said he would buy the plain new wheat. Ten days later Myren cut seventeen notches.

**Harold, the lord who nailed up the bounty.** He nailed the bounty to the board by the south gatehouse himself: five hundred gold coins, the estate west of the city, a knighthood. He did not lie about it. The gold was on Eartower's ledgers, and the ledgers had burned; the estate he would give if it had not burned too; the knighthood was his to give. He called everyone who came to take the bounty "sir" already: the title was not given yet, but his mouth gave it first. His son went with the first army, and only the horse came back; every morning after that he fed the empty-saddled horse himself, and the town called him the lord who feeds the riderless horse. He raised the second army and the third, forty-two and twenty-six, and never more: he wanted people who would climb onto the thing, and more men, he said, were only more kindling. He read every name aloud at the south gate before they left, though he could not read a word; he had them all by heart. He never once told Ronan to go, and Ronan never waited to be told. The morning he finally said it, it was too late.
